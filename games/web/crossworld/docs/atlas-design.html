The Atlas of Fifty Worlds

A daily word game where you don't solve the crossword — you write it. Fifty interconnected worlds, one per day, each one made permanent by the words you put into it. The fiftieth world is built from your own answers, so no two players ever finish the same game.

Worlds: 50 Regions: 7 × 7 + 1 Total slots: 388 Run length: ~50 days Platform: Devvit Web

1 The Premise

Everything below is built on the mechanic that already exists in the prototype. Nothing here requires replacing it.

In a normal crossword, a setter writes clues and you supply the answers. CrossWorld inverts that: the grid gives you a slot, and you supply both the answer and the clue. A judge decides whether your word is real and whether your clue is fair. If it passes, the word is written into the world permanently and the terrain around it wakes up.

The three checks, and who owns them

CheckOwnerWhat it enforces
FitServer, deterministicLength, crossing letters, A–Z only, answer not present in its own clue, 140-char cap.
RestrictionServer, deterministicThe one-use constraint you spent on this slot. Pure string/regex checks, never a judgment call.
MeaningLLM judgeOnly two booleans: validWord and fairClue. Nothing else.

This separation is load-bearing and must not erode. Every restriction added across all fifty worlds is mechanically checkable on the clue string. The judge never rules on style, difficulty, cleverness, or constraint compliance. That keeps the game fair, cheap, fast, and — critically — debuggable. A rejected clue always has a reason you can point at.

Why the player keeps coming back

The retention engine is not the puzzle. It is authorship. Every accepted entry is a sentence you wrote that is now part of a place. After fifty days you are not holding a score — you are holding roughly 388 clues you personally composed, attached to a map you personally routed. That is the artifact.

2 The Daily Loop

One world per day. A world is the unit of a session, not a slot.

01ArriveToday's world opens. You see its terrain and how many paths it holds.
02RevealOne discovery at a time: a slot's position, length, and any letters already fixed.
03SpendPick one unused restriction from today's deck. You cannot get it back.
04WriteSupply an answer that fits, and a clue that obeys the restriction.
05JudgeFit and restriction checked locally. Meaning checked by the judge (~1.7s).
06LockAccepted words are permanent. The terrain reacts. Next discovery opens.
07RouteWorld complete. It joins the atlas and reveals its neighbours. Pick tomorrow.

The daily allowance

  • One world per calendar day. Finishing early does not buy you the next world.
  • A world can be left mid-way and resumed the same day. Drafts already persist in the prototype; this extends to partial worlds.
  • Gate worlds are the one exception: they may straddle a day boundary without breaking the streak, because they run long by design.
  • Missing a day does not reset the atlas. It breaks a streak counter, nothing else. A fifty-day commitment cannot also be a fifty-day perfect-attendance requirement — that is a design trap.

The spend decision

A world's restriction deck is sized to its slot count, and every restriction is one-use. You choose which restriction to spend before you know what the later slots look like. Burning Two Breaths ("exactly 2 words") on an easy 4-letter noun feels efficient right up until the final slot turns out to be an 8-letter verb with five fixed crossings and Two Breaths is the only card you had left.

This is the core tactical tension and it scales for free. Bigger worlds mean bigger decks, longer planning horizons, and more ways to strand yourself. No new mechanic is needed to make world 40 harder than world 4.

3 Atlas Topology

Fifty worlds arranged as a connected graph, not a tree. Choice controls order, never exclusion.

Seven regions of seven, and the fiftieth

Regions are geographic, thematic, chromatic, and mechanical all at once. Each holds six open worlds and one Gate. The Gate unlocks only when the other six in its region are complete, and clearing it opens the next region.

The narrative shape of that sequence: washed up → cultivated → wild → made → high → remembered → unmade → remade. You arrive with nothing, and you leave having written the world back into existence.

Why "choose one of two" still matters when nothing is excluded

This is the design question that has to be answered honestly. If you eventually play all fifty worlds, a branch that doesn't remove anything is theatre. It only earns its place if order has consequences.

It does, for two reasons:

  1. Carry-forward makes order into difficulty. A world inherits fixed letters from whichever neighbours are already complete. Enter a world early with one finished neighbour and you get two or three fixed tiles. Come back to it last, with four finished neighbours, and you inherit a dozen. You are choosing, every single day, whether to take the easy approach now and leave a knot for later, or take the knot while you still have clean restrictions in hand.
  2. Restriction decks are region-scoped. The nastiest cards in a region's pool have to be spent somewhere inside that region. Routing is a budgeting problem: which world do you want to be holding No Repeats on?

The frontier shows 2 destinations in Regions I–III, 3 in Regions IV–VII, with the region's Gate appearing once the other six are lit.

Frontier rules

  • Completing a world reveals all of its unvisited neighbours as frontier.
  • Frontier worlds never expire and never disappear.
  • You may only enter a world adjacent to completed terrain — the atlas grows outward from Sandy Shore.
  • A Gate is adjacent to every world in its region, so it lights the moment the sixth one closes.

4 The Seven Axes

Each region introduces exactly one new source of difficulty, then carries it forward. By Region VII all seven are live simultaneously.

I Volume

The Shallows. Slot count and word length. Every discovery's Across and Down cross at one shared letter — the shipped behaviour — but discoveries stay independent of each other.

II Cross-discovery

The Hedge. Crossings reach between discoveries. Your third answer constrains your sixth, hours later in the same session.

III Carry-forward

The Hollow. Border tiles are inherited from completed neighbours. Words you wrote days ago start dictating letters.

IV Stacking

The Works. Two restrictions per slot, drawn from different tiers. Combinatorial pain, still fully deterministic.

V Compulsion

The Drift. Some slots arrive with their restriction pre-assigned. Choice is removed exactly where it hurts.

VI Meta-rules

The Reliquary. A world-wide constraint binds every clue in the session to every other one.

VII Subtraction

The Unmaking. Letters leave the board. Slots are pre-filled with wrong answers. The grid fights back.

Deliberately absent: a timer, a score, a leaderboard, and a lives system. None of them survive contact with a game whose core act is composition. A clock turns clue-writing into panic-typing, and a leaderboard rewards the shortest acceptable clue rather than the best one.

Breathers are not filler

Every region's breather is smaller and meaner — fewer slots, but a concentrated version of the region's axis. The Espalier (#11) is four slots that all cross each other. Thin Air (#32) is six slots where every single restriction is forced. They reset session length without resetting pressure.

These need playtest calibration more than anything else in this document. No Repeats on a 3-word clue is fine; on a 7-word clue it is impossible. Tier VI cards must only ever be dealt alongside a permissive shape card, and the deck builder has to enforce that pairing rather than trusting the content author.

Deck construction rules

Decks are per-world and thematically named. A card's mechanic is stable and global; its name belongs to the world it appears in. "Exactly two words" is Two Shells on Sandy Shore and Two Posts on The Long Pier — one deterministic check, two pieces of fiction. Implementations must key saved records on the mechanic id, never the display name.

Each world draws a distinct subset sized to its slot count, so a completed world consumes its deck exactly. Cards are one-use and are eliminated on acceptance — the first playtest shipped without this and the spend decision had no weight at all. A card is reserved on draft and spent only when a pair is accepted, so a rejected clue or an unavailable judge never consumes it.

7 Carry-Forward & The Solvability Guarantee

The single largest technical risk in this design. It has a clean answer, but the answer has to be built, not assumed.

How overlap works

The atlas is one continuous coordinate space. Worlds occupy adjacent rectangular regions on it. Where world B borders a completed world A, the letters in A's border cells become fixed seed tiles in B.

World A (done) World B (today)
· S H O R E · → E _ _ _ _
· · · · T · → T _ _ _
· · · · A · → A _ _ _ _ _

Seeds The last letter of each of A's border words becomes the first letter of a slot in B. Seeds are rendered in the neighbour world's accent colour and labelled with its name, so the player can see which past self caused today's problem.

The failure mode: a player writes a perfectly legal answer that leaves a later slot in the same world with a pattern no English word matches. The world becomes unwinnable, the daily allowance is spent, and the atlas is bricked. This will happen without an explicit defence — the player supplies the words, so there is no authored answer key to fall back on.

The Lantern Guarantee

Three layers, all deterministic, none involving the judge:

  1. Pre-offer check. A world is only offered as frontier if every slot in it, given its current seed tiles, matches at least K dictionary words. Run at route-reveal time against a precomputed pattern index.
  2. Pre-accept check. When a player submits an answer, before locking it, re-derive the patterns of every remaining slot in the world. If any drops to zero matches, reject with a specific message — "That answer closes the path to the fountain. Try another." This is a fit failure, not a judge failure, and it costs the player nothing.
  3. Loosen the Knot. Once per world, the player may release one inherited seed letter. The world stays winnable no matter what. It costs the world's clean clear mark on the atlas — a cosmetic, permanent, visible scar. Never a hard block.

RegionIIIIIIIVVVIVII50
Min matches6040251812853

K falling is itself a difficulty lever: by Region VII a slot may have only five legal words in the language, and finding one of them is the puzzle.

Cost of the pattern index. A 60k-word dictionary indexed by length and letter-position fits comfortably in memory and answers a pattern query in microseconds. Precompute at build time, ship as a static asset, keep it server-side so the client cannot enumerate valid answers. This is the one piece of genuinely new infrastructure the fifty-world design requires.

8 The Judge at Scale

388 judge calls per full playthrough. The prototype's judge is already correctly scoped; the concern is consistency, cost, and abuse.

What stays exactly as it is

The instruction block in src/server/judge.ts is well-built and should not be loosened. It already: treats submitted text as untrusted data rather than instructions, refuses to invent bans the server doesn't enforce, returns a strict two-boolean schema, and fails closed to JudgeUnavailable without consuming the player's restriction. Keep all of that.

What has to change for fifty worlds

ConcernAt 10 callsAt 388 callsResponse
ConsistencyInvisiblePlayers will find and share edge casesCache verdicts by (answer, normalised clue, judgeVersion). Identical submissions must always get identical rulings.
CostNegligible~388 × mini-model per completionStill cheap. Cache hits on common short clues will cut it further. Not a blocker.
Latency~1.7s, fine~11 min of cumulative waitingOptimistic UI: lock the tile visually on submit, reconcile on verdict. Never make the player watch a spinner 388 times.
AbuseBounded by 10/dayRetries are unboundedPer-user daily call ceiling well above honest play (~60), separate from the one-world-per-day rule.
DriftN/AModel updates change verdicts mid-runJUDGE_VERSION is already there. Pin the model. Never re-judge a locked entry — accepted is accepted, forever.

The one rule that protects a fifty-day run: a locked entry is never re-evaluated. Not on model upgrade, not on rule change, not on migration. A player who is thirty worlds deep must never log in to find that world 12 has been invalidated.

Region I · The Shallows

Worlds 1–7Axis: Volume

You wash up with nothing. Sun-bleached, gentle, and quietly wrong under the surface. Every discovery here is a real crossing — one Across, one Down, one shared letter — so the player learns the actual puzzle from day one. What grows across the region is how much of it you do at once.

Region I is a crossword from world 1. An earlier draft of this document specified "no crossings" here, which contradicted the shipped prototype — worlds.ts Sandy Shore already pairs an Across at (2,0,len 5) with a Down at (0,1,len 6) that meet at (2,1), and rules.ts validates the shared letter. Stripping crossings would make the first seven days a word-prompt worksheet and would invalidate Phase 1 as the kill gate: you cannot test whether an interlocking atlas is fun with a build that does not interlock. Every discovery pair crosses exactly once. 16 crossings across the region.

World 44 is the riskiest design in the document. Letting a player delete a letter from the permanent atlas is thematically perfect and mechanically dangerous — it can retroactively invalidate a completed word in another world. Rule: deletion only affects tiles that are not load-bearing crossings for any completed entry. The board must grey out illegal deletions rather than allowing and then rejecting them. If that constraint leaves too few legal targets to be interesting, cut the world rather than weakening the invariant.

The Fiftieth · The Cartographer's Table

World 5014 slots

The atlas folds down into a single grid. Every crossing letter in it was written by you, in one of the forty-nine worlds behind you, and every one is labelled with where it came from.

No two players ever play the same fiftieth world. The grid is generated from the player's own answer history — which means the finale is not content the team authored, it is content the player authored across seven weeks without realising it. That is the whole payoff of the fifty-day structure, and it is the reason the design is worth building at this scale.

How the grid is generated

  1. Take one accepted answer from each of the seven regions — the player's longest in each, ties broken by earliest.
  2. Lay those seven as the grid's spine, intersecting at shared letters. Colour each in its home region's accent.
  3. Generate fourteen open slots crossing that spine, verified solvable at K = 3.
  4. Hover any crossing letter to see the world, the date, and the clue you wrote for it.

Seven discoveries

The fold · Seven regions · Your first word · Your worst clue · The Long Pier returns · Every crossing · The Last Word

The restriction deck

Your seven most-frequently-spent cards across the whole atlas, dealt back to you. All fourteen slots are pre-assigned — there is no choosing left. The game hands you the constraints you leaned on most and asks you to use them one final time, which reads as either a gift or an indictment depending on how you played.

The Last Word

The final slot is eleven letters and crosses every region spine. It cannot be started until the other thirteen are locked. Its clue carries a single restriction: Bookends — the first and last word of the clue must be the same word. The sentence closes on itself, and so does the atlas.

9 Completion Payoff

Fifty days is a serious ask. The ending has to be worth the commitment, and it has to be shareable — this is Reddit.

1 The Atlas

All fifty worlds render as one continuous illustrated map, fully lit, each in its region's palette. Clean-cleared worlds glow; worlds where you loosened a knot carry a visible scar. Exportable as a single tall image.

2 Your Lexicon

All ~388 answers and the clues you wrote for them, dated, grouped by world. This is the real trophy. It is not a score — it is seven weeks of your own writing.

3 The Shareable

A generated Reddit post: your finished atlas, your Last Word, and three clues the game picked out as your best. Zero spoilers for anyone still playing — nobody else's grid matches yours.

4 The Second Atlas

New game plus. Same fifty worlds, one added rule: every answer you used the first time is banned. Costs no new content and is meaningfully harder.

Mid-run payoffs

Nobody plays fifty days on the promise of day fifty. Each of the seven Gates has to land as its own ending:

  • The region transforms. Clearing a Gate re-renders all seven of its worlds in their final, fully-lit state on the atlas.
  • A Region Card. A small illustrated card naming your best clue in that region, shareable on its own.
  • One restriction retires. You pick a card you never want to see again. It leaves your pool permanently. Small, personal, and it makes the next region feel like yours.

Seven endings, not one. A player who stops at world 21 should still feel they finished something. That is the difference between a fifty-day game and a fifty-day chore.

What has to change from the current prototype. Most of it is additive.

World definition — extends the existing shape

Today's WorldDefinition in src/shared/worlds.ts already carries id, scenario, apiRoot, name, chapter, noun, and a days[] array of across/down pairs. It needs region metadata, deck configuration, and atlas placement:

New: the pattern index

A build-time artifact, not a runtime service. Index a ~60k-word dictionary by (length, position, letter) into bitsets; a pattern query is a series of intersections. Ships as a static server-side asset. Never exposed to the client — otherwise the client can enumerate every legal answer and the game is over.

Carried forward unchanged

  • The validateTurn deterministic gate in src/shared/rules.ts — extended with new cards, same structure.
  • The judge contract, instruction block, timeout, and JudgeUnavailable fail-closed behaviour.
  • Draft persistence and the save-before-switch guard already built for the world picker.
  • Per-world scenario keys and API roots — these scale to fifty without change.

Fifty hand-authored worlds is the real cost, and it is not in the code. Each needs a grid layout, atlas placement, a verified-solvable slot set, discovery names, and palette work. Budget an authoring tool plus a validator that runs the Lantern Guarantee across all fifty at build time. Hand-checking fifty grids will not hold.

12 Risks & Open Questions

RiskSeverityMitigation
Fifty-day retention. Daily word games lose most players in week one. Fifty days is a very long tail.HighSeven Gate endings. No streak-reset punishment. Region I is under 7 minutes a day.
Clue-writing fatigue. 388 original clues is far more composition than any comparable game asks for.HighBreathers, the ramp, and a hard 10-slot cap on non-Gate worlds. Watch abandon rate by slot index, not by world.
Carry-forward deadlock. A legal answer strands a later slot.MedSolved by design — pre-offer check, pre-accept check, Loosen the Knot. Requires the pattern index to exist first.
Authoring cost. Fifty grids, 388 verified slots, 50 palettes, 175 discovery names.MedGenerator plus build-time validator. Names and premises are already written in this document.
Judge inconsistency. Over 388 calls, players will find contradictory rulings and post them.MedVerdict caching, pinned model, never re-judge a locked entry.
Tier VI calibration. Cruel cards may be outright impossible on some slots.MedDeck builder enforces pairing with a permissive shape card. Needs playtest data, not more design.
Routing is theatre. If order consequences aren't legible, branching feels fake.HighSeeds are colour-coded and attributed to the world that caused them. The frontier preview shows inherited-letter count before you commit.

Open questions

  1. Is a world ever replayable? Right now a completed world is frozen. A player who writes a clue they're unhappy with has no recourse for fifty days. A once-per-region "revise one clue" token would fix that — but it weakens permanence, which is the emotional core.
  2. Should other players' clues ever be visible? This is a Reddit game and there is an obvious social layer: see how three strangers clued the same slot. It is also the single fastest way to turn authorship into imitation. Recommend keeping it out of v1 and revisiting after Phase 4.
  3. What happens on day 51? The Second Atlas is a cheap answer. A better one may be player-authored worlds — but that is a content-moderation surface, not a feature.
  4. Does the atlas need to be geographically coherent? The design assumes a literal 2D map. A more abstract constellation would be cheaper to author and lose some of the "one growing world" feeling. Worth prototyping both in Phase 1.
